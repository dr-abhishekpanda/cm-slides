Pneumonia: A Leading Killer of Under-5s

~15%of under-5 deaths in India are due to pneumonia

Most childhood ARI is upper respiratory and self-limiting; deaths come from pneumonia, mainly S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae type b, and RSV.

Risk Factors
  • Low birth weight, malnutrition
  • Not exclusively breastfed
  • Indoor air pollution (chulha smoke), parental smoking
  • Crowding; no measles or Hib/PCV vaccination
  • Vitamin A and zinc deficiency

0:03–0:11. GAPPD framework: Protect (breastfeeding, nutrition), Prevent (vaccines, clean air, handwashing), Treat (case management, amoxicillin, oxygen).

Is This Fast Breathing?

Count for a full minute when the child is calm.

0:11–0:16. Cut-offs: under 2 months ≥ 60; 2–12 months ≥ 50; 12 months to 5 years ≥ 40. In young infants, a repeat count is needed if the first is 60 or more.

Classify and Treat (2–59 Months)

Severe Pneumonia

Cough or difficult breathing + any general danger sign (not able to drink, vomits everything, convulsions, lethargic or unconscious) or stridor in a calm child, or severe malnutrition.
First dose of antibiotic; refer urgently.

Pneumonia

Fast breathing and/or chest indrawing, no danger sign.
Oral amoxicillin (5 days); follow up in 2–3 days.

No Pneumonia: Cough or Cold

No fast breathing, no chest indrawing.
Soothe the throat, keep feeding; no antibiotic; return if worse.

0:16–0:26. In the 2014 WHO revision, chest indrawing without danger signs moved from "severe" to "pneumonia" treated with oral amoxicillin, except in HIV-exposed children (check your state's IMNCI chart). Young infants under 2 months with fast breathing or severe chest indrawing are referred (possible serious bacterial infection).

Preventing Childhood Pneumonia

Vaccines

PCV at 6 weeks, 14 weeks and booster at 9 months (UIP); Hib in pentavalent; measles; pertussis

Protect

Exclusive breastfeeding; adequate complementary feeding; vitamin A; zinc

Clean Air

LPG instead of chulha; no smoking at home; ventilation

SAANS (2019): community awareness, ASHA training to count breaths, amoxicillin and pulse oximetry at every level, observed every November–February.

0:31–0:37. PCV was introduced in phases from 2017 and scaled nationally in 2021.

Carriers, outbreaks and chemoprophylaxis.

Epidemiology of Meningococcal Disease

AgentNeisseria meningitidis; serogroups A, B, C, W, X, Y
ReservoirNasopharyngeal carriers (far outnumber cases)
SpreadDroplets; close prolonged contact
Incubation3–4 days (2–10)
SettingCrowding: hostels, barracks, camps, pilgrimages
Recognise Early

Fever, headache, neck stiffness, vomiting; petechial or purpuric rash that does not blanch. Fulminant meningococcaemia can kill within hours.

0:37–0:44. Serogroup A caused major epidemics in the African meningitis belt and outbreaks in India (e.g. Delhi 2005). Treatment: ceftriaxone or penicillin as early as possible.

Protect the Close Contacts

Who Is a Close Contact?

Household members, hostel or dormitory mates, kissing contacts, and health workers exposed to secretions (e.g. intubation) within 7 days before onset

Vaccines

Polysaccharide and conjugate vaccines (A, C, W, Y; MenAfriVac for A). Used in outbreaks and for Hajj and Umrah pilgrims; not in the UIP.

0:44–0:52. Give prophylaxis within 24 hours of identifying the case. Check doses against current NCDC guidance.

Take-Home

  1. Count breaths for a full minute: 60 · 50 · 40 by age.
  2. Danger signs = severe pneumonia: first dose and refer.
  3. Pneumonia without danger signs: oral amoxicillin at home, follow-up.
  4. Prevent with breastfeeding, clean air, PCV, Hib, measles vaccines.
  5. Meningococcal disease: carriers spread it; chemoprophylaxis for close contacts.
