Four Uses of Screening

a · Prescriptive screening

Case detection

People screened for their own benefit. Neonatal screening; bacteriuria in pregnancy; cervical cancer; PKU.

b · Prospective screening

Control of disease

People examined for the benefit of others. Immigrants screened for TB and syphilis; streptococcal infection to prevent rheumatic fever.

c

Research purposes

Initial screening gives prevalence; later rounds give incidence. Participants must be told no follow-up therapy will be available.

d

Educational opportunities

Builds public awareness and educates health professionals, e.g. diabetes screening camps.

0:20–0:23. Exam tip: "prescriptive" vs "prospective" is a favourite MCQ. Memory hook: pre-SCRIPT-ive = a prescription for me; pro-SPECT-ive = protecting the spectators.

Three Types of Screening

Mass

Whole population or a sub-group (e.g. all adults), irrespective of individual risk. Of little value unless backed by effective treatment.

High-risk / selective

Applied to groups at high risk, defined by epidemiological research. Most productive. Extends to screening for risk factors, e.g. serum cholesterol for CHD.

Multiphasic

Two or more tests in combination, at one time. Popular, but RCTs in the UK and USA showed no reduction in mortality or morbidity, at higher cost.

(Population-based, i.e. mass screening of an age-defined sub-group; it is also multiphasic since BP, glucose and three cancers are screened together.) Which type is NSCAEM? (Selective: tribal-majority areas, age 0–40.)

A Good Screening Test Is Acceptable, Repeatable and Valid

1

Acceptability

A high rate of cooperation is needed. Painful, discomforting or embarrassing tests (rectal, vaginal examination) are less acceptable in mass campaigns.

2

Repeatability

Consistent results when repeated on the same person under the same conditions. Also called reliability, precision, reproducibility.

3

Validity

Ability to separate those who have the disease from those who do not. Components: sensitivity and specificity.

Plus: yield, simplicity, safety, rapidity, ease of administration and cost. The choice of test is often a compromise.

Three Sources of Variation

A · Observer variation

Intra-observer: same observer, same subject, different result.

Inter-observer: one reader finds malaria parasites on a slide; a second calls it normal.

B · Biological variation

BP, blood sugar and cholesterol fluctuate in the same person.

Regression to the mean: extreme values drift towards the average on repeat measurement.

C · Technical methods

Defective instruments, wrong calibration, faulty reagents, or an unsuitable test.

8

Sensitivity · Yerushalmy, 1940s
"the ability of a test to identify correctly all those who have the disease, that is 'true-positive'."

High sensitivity → few false negatives → fewer people falsely reassured.

Specificity
"the ability of a test to identify correctly those who do not have the disease, that is, 'true-negatives'."

High specificity → few false positives → fewer healthy people sent for work-up.

Did Screening Actually Help?

Methods of Evaluation
  • RCT: the ideal; costly for rare diseases with long latency, may need tens of thousands followed for 10–20 years
  • Uncontrolled trials: e.g. survival after cervical cancer screening
  • Other: case-control studies; trends in areas with different screening coverage
Biases That Flatter Screening
  • Lead-time bias: survival looks longer only because diagnosis came earlier
  • Length bias: screening picks up slow-growing disease more often
  • Volunteer bias: those who attend differ from those who do not
  • Overdiagnosis: disease that would never have caused harm
