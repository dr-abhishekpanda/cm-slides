Water II · The Vital Layer

The Vital Layer

"The vital layer is the 'heart' of the slow sand filter."
  • Also called Schmutzdecke, zoogleal or biological layer
  • Slimy, gelatinous: algae, plankton, diatoms, bacteria
  • Forms over several days: "ripening" of the filter. The first filtrate goes to waste.
  • Removes organic matter, holds back bacteria, oxidises ammoniacal nitrogen to nitrates
  • Cleaning: scrape the top 1–2 cm when the loss of head grows

A working slow sand filter cuts total bacterial count by 99.9–99.99% and E. coli by 99–99.9%.

Water II · Rapid Sand Filter

Rapid Sand Filter: Step by Step

  1. Coagulation: alum, 5–40 mg/L or more, by turbidity, colour, temperature and pH
  2. Rapid mixing: violent agitation for a few minutes
  3. Flocculation: slow stirring for about 30 minutes (paddles at 2–4 rpm); a white floc of aluminium hydroxide forms
  4. Sedimentation: 2–6 hours; at least 95% of the floc must be removed
  5. Filtration: sand of effective size 0.4–0.7 mm; 5–15 m³/m²/hour
  6. Backwashing: reverse flow when loss of head nears 7–8 feet; about 15 minutes

0:15–0:19. Types: gravity (Paterson's) and pressure (Candy's). Advantages over slow sand: deals with raw water directly, less space, 40–50 times faster, easy washing, flexible.

Water II · The Ideal Disinfectant

What Should a Water Disinfectant Do?

  1. Destroy pathogens within the available contact time, whatever the temperature, pH and minerals
  2. Leave no toxic products, colour or taste
  3. Be available, affordable, and safe and accurate to apply
  4. Leave a residual to deal with small recontamination
  5. Be measurable by a quick, simple test

Chlorine meets all five. It also oxidises iron, manganese and H₂S, removes some tastes and odours, controls algae and aids coagulation.

0:23–0:24. Chlorine has no effect on spores and certain viruses (polio, hepatitis) except in high doses.

Water II · Principles of Chlorination

The Mere Addition of Chlorine Is Not Chlorination

  1. Water must be clear: turbidity impedes chlorination
  2. Estimate the chlorine demand
  3. Allow a contact period of at least 1 hour
  4. Minimum free residual chlorine: 0.5 mg/L for 1 hour
  5. Correct dose = chlorine demand + 0.5 mg/L free residual
"The chlorine demand of water is the difference between the amount of chlorine added to the water, and the amount of residual chlorine remaining at the end of a specific period of contact (usually 60 minutes), at a given temperature and pH of the water."

0:27–0:30. Chlorine does not kill spores, protozoal cysts or helminthic ova except in higher doses.

Water II · Breakpoint Chlorination

Finding the Breakpoint

  1. Demand by reducing agents: no residual
  2. Chlorine + ammonia → chloramines (combined residual)
  3. More chlorine destroys chloramines: residual falls
  4. Breakpoint: all combined chlorine destroyed
  5. Beyond it, free residual rises with every added dose

0:30–0:33. Breakpoint chlorination is "controlled superchlorination". Superchlorination followed by dechlorination suits heavily polluted water of fluctuating quality. The curve is schematic, not to scale.

Water II · Methods and Tests

Applying and Measuring Chlorine

For Large Volumes
  • Chlorine gas: first choice; cheap, quick, efficient. Needs a chlorinating device (e.g. Paterson's chloronome)
  • Chloramines: less taste, persistent residual, but slow
  • Perchloron (high-test hypochlorite): 60–70% available chlorine
To Measure the Residual
  • Orthotolidine (OT) test: yellow colour; read free chlorine within 10 seconds; colour after 15–20 min = free + combined
  • OTA test: separates free and combined residuals; avoids errors from nitrites, iron and manganese

Note: the orthotolidine reagent is now restricted in many countries as a suspected carcinogen; DPD kits are the common field alternative.

0:33–0:36. Point to verify: whether your state's field kits still use OT. Many CPHEEO and NHM field kits use DPD (N,N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine) or chlorine test strips.

Water II · Household Methods

Household Purification

Boiling

Rolling Boil, 10–20 min

Kills bacteria, spores, cysts and ova; removes temporary hardness. No residual protection: store in the same pot.

Chemical

Chlorine Compounds

Bleaching powder (33% available chlorine, unstable), chlorine solution, HTH (60–70%), chlorine tablets (0.5 g for 20 L). Iodine for emergencies.

Physical

Filters, UV, RO

Ceramic candles (Chamberland, Berkefeld, Katadyn) stop bacteria, not viruses. UV at 254 nm. Multistage RO.

Potassium permanganate is no longer recommended: it may kill cholera vibrios but is of little use against other organisms, and it alters colour, smell and taste.

0:37–0:41. Iodine: two drops of 2% ethanolic solution per litre of clear water, 20–30 minutes' contact. Ceramic candles need weekly scrubbing and boiling.

Water II · Disinfecting a Well

Disinfect the Village Well

Volume (L) = 3.14 × d² × h ÷ 4 × 1,000

Water in the Well
Bleaching Powder

  1. Find the volume of water
  2. Find chlorine demand (Horrock's apparatus); roughly 2.5 g per 1,000 L
  3. Make a paste, ≤100 g per bucket; let lime settle 5–10 min; use the supernatant
  4. Lower the bucket below the surface and agitate
  5. Allow 1 hour contact
  6. Test: free residual ≥ 0.5 mg/L, else repeat

0:41–0:47. Back to the opening case. Disinfect at night after the day's draw-off; during cholera epidemics, every day. Do not pour the lime sediment into the well (raises hardness). 2.5 g/1,000 L gives about 0.7 mg/L applied chlorine.

Water II · Double Pot Method

The Double Pot: Steady Chlorine for 2–3 Weeks

  • Devised by NEERI, Nagpur
  • Outer pot: 30 cm high, 25 cm diameter; hole 4 cm above the bottom
  • Inner pot: hole near the rim; filled to 3 cm below the hole with moistened bleaching powder and coarse sand
  • Lowered 1 m below water level in a well of about 4,500 L with a draw-off of 360–450 L/day
  • Two pots allow larger holes without over-chlorination

0:47–0:50. The double pot suits emergencies where daily chlorination is not possible.

Water II · Take-Home

Take-Home

  1. Storage → filtration → disinfection: storage alone cuts bacteria by 90%.
  2. Slow sand filters work biologically; the vital layer is the heart.
  3. Rapid sand filters need alum, flocculation and backwashing.
  4. Chlorine works best near pH 7; aim for 0.5 mg/L free residual after 1 hour.
  5. Wells: about 2.5 g bleaching powder per 1,000 L, one hour's contact, then test.
