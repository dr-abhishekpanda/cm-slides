Planning Disease Control · Control to Eradication

Control, Elimination, Eradication

Control

"(ongoing) operations aimed at reducing: the incidence of disease, the duration of disease, and consequently the risk of transmission, the effects of infection… and the financial burden to the community"

Elimination

"interruption of transmission of disease, as for example, elimination of measles, polio and diphtheria from large geographic regions or areas"

Eradication

"termination of all transmission of infection by extermination of the infectious agent": an "all or none phenomenon", worldwide. Only smallpox so far.

In control, the agent persists "at a level where it ceases to be a public health problem".

0:03–0:07. Park adds that "residual" infection with hidden foci is why eradication campaigns against malaria, yaws, plague, kala-azar and yellow fever disappointed. WHO now also uses "elimination as a public health problem" for targets short of zero transmission (kala-azar, leprosy, lymphatic filariasis, trachoma).

Park 27e, Ch 2: Concepts of control

Planning Disease Control · Planning Cycle

The Planning Cycle

0:10–0:15. Park defines planning as "a process of analysing a system, or defining a problem, assessing the extent to which the problem exists as a need, formulating goals and objectives…, examining and choosing from among alternative intervention strategies, initiating the necessary action for its implementation and monitoring… and evaluating the results of intervention in the light of stated objectives." Park's Fig. 1 has more boxes (assign priorities among objectives, design alternative programmes, select the best by feasibility and cost-effectiveness, orient and train personnel); this is a simplified version.

Planning Disease Control · Set Priorities

Step 3: Set Priorities in the Open

PEARL: Propriety · Economics · Acceptability · Resources · Legality

Priority Score (0–100)

Score = (A + 2B) × C ÷ 3. Tap a PEARL letter to mark it "No"; tap again for "Yes".

0:20–0:28. Hanlon's basic priority rating weights seriousness twice. PEARL (Propriety, Economics, Acceptability, Resources, Legality) is a yes/no screen: any "No" sets the problem aside for now, whatever its score. The presets use invented scores for the opening block; ask groups to score the same problems and compare. The value is in making choices explicit, not in the decimals. Park's planning cycle simply says "assign priorities among problems"; Hanlon is one transparent way to do it.

Hanlon method (Hanlon and Pickett, 1984), as presented in the NACCHO Guide to Prioritization Techniques

Planning Disease Control · Objectives

Step 4: Make the Objective SMART

Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. A goal is the ultimate desired state; a target counts an activity.

0:28–0:33. Park: "An OBJECTIVE (point) is precise - it is either achieved or not achieved." "A TARGET often refers to a discrete activity such as the number of blood films collected or vasectomies done; it permits the concept of degree of achievement." "GOAL is defined as the ultimate desired state towards which objectives and resources are directed." The numbers in the improved objectives are illustrative.

Step 6–7: Make It Happen

Work Plan

Who does what, where and by when; a simple timeline (Gantt chart); a budget in the PIP

People and Supplies

Planning Disease Control · Three Words

Monitoring, Surveillance, Evaluation

Monitoring

"the performance and analysis of routine measurements aimed at detecting changes in the environment or health status of population"

Surveillance

"Systematic and continuous collection, analysis, and interpretation of data, closely integrated with the timely and coherent dissemination of the results… so that action can be taken"

Evaluation

"the process by which results are compared with the intended objectives, or more simply the assessment of how well a programme is performing"

Monitoring tends to be episodic; surveillance is continuous and ends in action.

0:43–0:46. In management, monitoring is also "the episodic oversight of the implementation of an activity", checking that inputs, schedules and outputs run to plan. Sentinel surveillance supplements routine notification. In India, IDSP reports on S (syndromic), P (presumptive) and L (laboratory-confirmed) forms through IHIP.

Park 27e, Ch 2: Concepts of control (monitoring, surveillance, evaluation)
Planning Disease Control · Indicators

From Input to Impact: Sort the Indicators

0:46–0:50. Park's three types of evaluation map onto this chain: structure (facilities, staff, equipment), process (how activities are carried out against a standard), and outcome (the "5 Ds": disease, discomfort, dissatisfaction, disability and death). Process indicators tell you early whether you are on track; impact takes seasons or years.

Logic-model indicator chain; Donabedian 1966 (structure, process, outcome); Park 27e, Ch 23

Planning Disease Control · Early Warning

Is This Week Unusual?

Threshold = Mean + 2 SD

0:50–0:53. A simple, widely taught threshold: compare this week's count with the mean plus two standard deviations of the same weeks in previous years (excluding past epidemic years). Above it, verify the data and investigate. IDSP also treats clusters of cases or deaths in one place as triggers. Thresholds work only if reporting is complete and timely every week.

CDC Principles of Epidemiology (epidemic thresholds); NCDC IDSP/IHIP

Planning Disease Control · Evaluate

Step 8: Evaluate Against the Objectives

  1. Determine what is to be evaluated
  2. Establish standards and criteria
  3. Plan the methodology
  4. Gather information
  5. Analyse the results
  6. Take action
  7. Re-evaluate

0:53–0:56. Park's seven elements of evaluation: relevance, adequacy (enough attention and staff for the workload), accessibility, acceptability (for example male sterilisation), effectiveness, efficiency and impact (overall effect on health and development, as when malaria control improved agriculture and industry). Evaluation "should always be considered during the planning and implementation stages".

Planning Disease Control · Take-Home

Take-Home

  1. Control, elimination and eradication are different end-points.
  2. Plan in a cycle: data → priorities → objectives → strategy → action → evaluation.
  3. Set priorities openly (Hanlon + PEARL); write SMART objectives.
  4. Choose interventions along the natural history; involve other sectors.
  5. Monitor processes, watch thresholds, evaluate against objectives.
